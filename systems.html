How the agent runner is governed

AI coding agents wrote the code behind my 45 products. I designed everything around them: the brief, the limits, the review and the decision to ship. Six controls, in the order a job meets them, and a job can be refused at any one of them.

  1. Written brief. Every job starts from a document I wrote, never from a chat: the goal, the scope and the budget.
  2. Acceptance test. The brief carries the test that defines done. A job is finished when the test passes, not when the agent says so.
  3. Allow-list. Each agent runs with a short list of permitted actions. Anything not on the list is refused, not asked about, and a person approves anything irreversible.
  4. Hard budget. A job takes its budget before it starts; when the budget is spent, nothing new launches.
  5. Review, then my release call. Tests and a bug-class scan gate every change. Nothing ships without my decision.
  6. Audit trail. Every action, refusal and approval goes on an append-only, hash-chained record, so after an incident the log answers what ran, whether it was allowed and who signed off.

Every control is proven by planting the failure it exists to catch. A control nobody has seen fail is not counted.

Scale and structure

Since April 2026 the runner has completed more than 10,000 governed agent runs on two servers I manage. Those runs built the 45 products and keep them running. Across every logged session since July 2026 that is roughly 45 billion tokens of model context in more than 5,400 sessions.

Several coordinating sessions dispatch agents through one shared budget and one shared rulebook. Idle capacity flows to whichever line is busy, and a stalled job can never hold the others' budget.
